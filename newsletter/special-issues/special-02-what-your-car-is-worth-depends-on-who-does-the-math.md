# What Your Car Is Worth Depends on Who Does the Math

*A special issue. Free, unpaywalled, and written from the passenger seat of a rental.*

## Intro

On a Tuesday afternoon in October, a commercial delivery truck crossed into my side of a residential road and dragged itself down the left side of my car, from the headlight to the back door. Nobody was hurt. The car was two months from paid off, one owner, every mile mine.

By dinner my insurer had called it a total loss, and I had a number in my head for what that meant.

My number was $12,000.

The evidence said something closer to $21,000.

Same car. Same damage. Same afternoon. The only thing that changed between those two numbers was about an hour of homework. This issue is that hour, so you don't have to do it on the worst day of your month.

## Signal vs. Noise

> **The Noise:** "Don't worry, that's what insurance is for. They'll make you whole."

It's the most comforting sentence in personal finance, and it's not wrong, exactly. It just leaves out the mechanism.

"Whole," in a property claim, means a number: the car's *actual cash value* right before the crash. Somebody has to calculate it. In practice that somebody is a valuation report, usually produced by third-party software, built from comparable vehicles, adjusted for mileage and condition, and handed to an adjuster whose job is to close the file at a defensible figure.

None of that is sinister. It's a process. But a process has inputs, and whoever supplies better inputs gets a better output. If the comparables are higher-mileage cars, a different trim, or a few hundred miles away, the number drops. If your condition rating defaults to "average" when you have every maintenance receipt since day one, the number drops. If nobody asks about sales tax and title fees, they may never come up.

The insurer isn't hiding the ball. It's waiting to see whether you know where it is.

> **The Signal:** you won't be made whole by default. You'll be paid what you can prove. The difference between those two sentences is the size of the check.

## The Number

**$10,650 vs. $19,700.**

The first is the published trade-in value for my exact car: a 2019 compact luxury sedan, base trim, as listed by a major pricing guide in October 2026. The second is the highest asking price among the closest comparable cars for sale within a few hundred miles at the same time.

The gap is about $9,000, and it isn't a typo. It's the difference between what a dealer would *pay* you for the car and what it would *cost* you to replace it. A total-loss settlement is supposed to be the second one, because the point is to put you back in an equivalent car, not to buy yours at a discount.

My gut said $12,000 because $12,000 *sounded* like what an eight-year-old car is worth. That's the trade-in instinct, and it's the one number you never want to anchor on in a total loss.

What would change its meaning: asking prices aren't sale prices, and the adjuster will rightly argue some of them down. The honest landing zone sits below the top listing. It doesn't sit anywhere near trade-in.

## The hour of homework

Here's what I actually did, in order.

**1. Pulled the comparables myself.** Same year, same trim, same transmission, mileage as close to mine as I could find, and as close to home as possible. A year older or newer works as a bracket.

| Comparable | Miles | Asking price |
|---|---|---|
| One year older, same model, base | ~55,000 | $18,299 |
| Same year, base | ~44,000 | $19,344 |
| Same year, base | ~58,000 | $19,707 |
| Same year, base | ~69,000 | $19,027 |
| One year newer, base | ~64,000 | $19,480 |

*Dealer asking prices, October 2026. Mine had about 53,000 miles.*

My car sat squarely in that band: younger than the older car, lower mileage than the newer one.

**2. Separated the four values everyone confuses.** A pricing guide will show you several numbers for the same car. For mine, at "typical mileage":

- **Trade-in:** $10,650. What a dealer pays you.
- **Private party:** $16,000. What a stranger pays you.
- **Dealer retail:** $17,200. What a dealer charges at typical mileage.
- **Actual comparables at my mileage:** about $19,000 to $19,700.

Only the last two describe the cost of *replacing* the car. "Typical mileage" for an eight-year-old car is well above mine, which pushes my number up, not down.

**3. Added the line items people forget.** In my state, a total-loss settlement may include the sales or excise tax you'd pay to buy the replacement (6% where I live, about $1,180 on a $19,700 car), plus title and registration fees. Ask whether yours does, and get the answer in writing. Recent receipts count too: I'd put a brand-new set of tires on it.

**4. Built the demand line by line.** Not a round number, an itemized one: vehicle value from the comps, plus tax, plus fees, plus receipts. Every line has a source. An adjuster can wave off a big round number. Six documented lines are much harder to dismiss.

**5. Disclosed the flaws myself.** My car had one known issue: an exhaust heat shield I'd removed for a rattle, with the replacement parts sitting in the trunk. I told them before they found it. A small deduction costs less than a credibility problem.

## Why a car with a crumpled side counts as "totaled"

People are often shocked that damage they can walk around is enough to total a car. Mine looked like a bad door ding from ten feet away.

Most states set a threshold: when repair costs reach a set share of the car's value (in my state, generally 75%), the car is a total loss. On a car worth about $19,000, that's roughly $14,000 of repairs, and modern bodywork gets there fast. Two doors, a fender, a bumper, a headlight, a mirror, suspension parts you can't see, paint blended across the whole side, and sensor recalibration.

Here's the part nobody tells you: on a well-kept car, a total is often the *better* financial outcome. A repaired car carries a permanent accident history and sells for less forever. A total pays market value in cash.

## The options nobody mentions

**You may be able to keep the car.** Most insurers allow "owner retention": they pay you the actual cash value *minus* what the car would bring at salvage auction, and you keep it with a salvage title. For anyone who wrenches, that's a parts car or a rebuild at a known price. Get the salvage figure in writing before you decide, and figure out where the car will live, because storage fees can eat the deal.

**If you're on your own policy, you may have an appraisal clause.** If you and your insurer can't agree on value, each side hires an appraiser and a neutral umpire settles it. It's in the policy for exactly this situation, and most people never read far enough to find it.

**If the other driver was at fault, you still get paid first.** Your insurer can pay you under your collision coverage, then pursue the at-fault driver's insurer to recover it, including your deductible. That's called subrogation. It's usually the fastest route to a check.

**"Whole" has limits.** A property claim generally pays market value, loss of use (a rental), and documented incidental costs. It doesn't pay for sentimental value, eight years of memories, or how much you liked the car. That gap is real. It's just not a line item.

## What to do the day your car is totaled

1. **Don't say a number first.** Ask for the full valuation report, including the comparables and every adjustment.
2. **Pull your own comps.** Same year, trim, and mileage band, as close to home as possible.
3. **Know which value you're looking at.** Trade-in is not replacement cost. Replacement cost is the point.
4. **Ask about tax, title, and fees.** In writing.
5. **Gather receipts:** maintenance records, tires, recent repairs. Condition is a negotiable input.
6. **Itemize your counter.** Every line gets a source.
7. **Read your policy** for an appraisal clause before you accept anything.
8. **Decide on retention** before the tow truck decides for you.

None of this requires a lawyer, a contact at the insurer, or any special access. It requires an hour and the willingness to check before you agree. That's the whole thesis of this newsletter, in a parking space.
