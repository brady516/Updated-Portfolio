# I Bought a Car by Text Message. The Seller Who Knew the Most Won.

## Intro *(free)*

I just bought a car I've never touched.

It sits at a dealership about 370 miles from my house. I read its entire life story in a vehicle history report. A salesman I've never met put it on a lift and texted me photos of the underside. I made one offer by text. An hour later, a buyer's order showed up in my inbox, itemized to the dollar, with the seller's signature already on it.

I've bought cars for 20 years. I've never bought one this way. What surprised me wasn't the technology. It was how clearly the process showed what I was actually paying for.

I wasn't paying for a car. I was paying for certainty about a car.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Never pay the asking price. Grind the dealer down."

It's the oldest advice in car buying. The dealer is the opponent, every dollar off is a win, and whoever pushes hardest gets the best deal.

That week I had two nearly identical cars in front of me: same model, a year apart, similar miles, priced within a few hundred dollars of each other once tax and fees were in.

One came from the dealer that sold it new and serviced it 20 times. They knew everything about it and showed me everything without being asked twice.

The other was a trade-in on a lot that sells a different brand. Its history report showed reported damage and no detail. When I asked what kind, the salesman guessed. When I asked for underbody photos, he grumbled.

I offered the second dealer a price that accounted for not knowing. They said no, and I walked. I offered the first dealer a few percent under their price and they accepted my opening offer. The harder grind happened on the car I didn't buy.

> **The Signal:** in used cars, most of the price is the price of uncertainty. The seller who can remove it gets paid for it. The seller who can't has to discount or wait.

## The Number *(free)*

**19%.** That's the share of all car buyers who used AI tools such as ChatGPT or AI-powered search summaries during their purchase, according to Cox Automotive's 2025 Car Buyer Journey study (published Jan. 19, 2026). Among new-car buyers it was 25%.

I was one of them. I used an AI assistant to price every car all-in, read history reports, check the buyer's order line by line, and draft my texts.

Cox found that among mostly digital buyers, 81% of AI users trusted they got a good deal, compared with 67% of non-users. That's the number that made me stop and think. AI didn't find me a cheaper car. It made it cheap for me to ask the questions that expose uncertainty, and that's what the dealers' prices were built on.

What would change its meaning: it's a survey of buyers' own reports, and "used an AI tool" covers everything from a search summary to a full negotiation. Feeling you got a good deal isn't the same as getting one. And AI tools can be confidently wrong, so everything they produce needs checking against the documents.

## The Read — used-car prices are information prices *(paid)*

**Thesis:** a used car isn't a commodity. Every one has a different history, and the buyer can see only part of it. In my view, the price of a used car is mostly a price on what the buyer doesn't know, and the seller who closes that gap earns the premium.

### The mechanism

Economists have studied this since George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons.'" When sellers know more than buyers, buyers assume the worst and pay less, and good cars get priced like bad ones. Here's what that looked like from my side of the text thread.

**1. Every used car is a one-off.** Two cars with the same year, model and miles can be completely different assets. One was dealer-serviced on schedule. The other was in an accident nobody will describe. A listing can't tell them apart. Only records can.

**2. Who knows the car decides who can sell certainty.** The dealer that sold the car new had its whole history in their own service system. Putting it on a lift cost them ten minutes. The dealer holding someone else's trade-in had a one-line damage note and nothing else. They couldn't reduce my uncertainty even if they'd wanted to. They could only price around it, or hope I didn't ask.

**3. "Uncertainty" is an objection you can only answer with evidence.** A lower price doesn't answer it, and neither does a feature list. When I asked what the damage was, the answer was a guess: "probably a scuff in the city." A real record usually lists the date, the area hit and a severity rating. A guess instead of a record told me more than the record would have.

**4. Text messages work because documents travel.** Buying remotely used to mean trusting a stranger. Now the trust sits in the paperwork: the history report, the lift photos, the itemized buyer's order. If the documents are good, the distance doesn't matter. If they're missing, no amount of in-person charm replaces them.

### The playbook I used

Observation, not instruction. This is what I did, in order:

1. **Read the history report before anything else.** Number of owners, accidents or damage, service records, how the car was used (personal, lease, fleet or rental).
2. **Ask for underbody photos on a lift.** A dealer with a clean car says yes. Watch how they say it.
3. **Price everything all-in.** Car, doc fee, your home state's tax and title, and the cost of getting there. Two cars priced the same can land hundreds apart.
4. **Make one clean offer in writing,** with "no other dealer fees" spelled out. My first listing carried a "fuel charge" and a "nitrogen tire fill." Neither made it to the final paperwork.
5. **Negotiate only the out-the-door price.** Not the monthly payment and not the down payment. Those only tell the seller where your limit is.
6. **Get a buyer's order and read every line,** including the terms on the back page, before you sign.
7. **Confirm who handles your home-state title and tax,** and get proof it was paid, so you're not charged twice.
8. **Put down only a small, refundable deposit** until you've seen the car. The down payment goes in at signing.

### The fair case for the other side

Kicking the tires still matters:

- **Photos are curated.** A seller picks the angles. An in-person inspection, ideally by an independent mechanic, catches what a camera doesn't.
- **Some uncertainty is a bargain.** A car with minor reported damage can be perfectly fine, and the discount is the whole point. I wasn't saying never buy a damaged car. I was saying don't pay clean-car money for one.
- **Not every fee is junk.** Doc fees and state inspection fees cover real paperwork. The trick is knowing which ones are optional.
- **AI tools make mistakes.** Mine got an owner count wrong in a draft text before I caught it. The documents are the authority, not the assistant.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **Documented cars stop commanding a premium.** If dealer-serviced, clean-history cars sell for the same as cars with gaps or damage, then buyers aren't pricing uncertainty and my thesis is weak.
2. **Remote purchases go wrong more often.** If buyers who buy sight unseen file more complaints and returns than in-person buyers, the documents aren't carrying the trust I think they are.
3. **Sellers stop sharing records.** If dealers learn that transparency costs them negotiating room and stop offering lift photos and service histories, the advantage shifts back to whoever has the information.

The honest bottom line: this is one purchase, one week, two dealers. But the pattern is as old as Akerlof. Information is worth money, and this time I could see exactly how much.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction:

1. **Cox Automotive's next Car Buyer Journey study.** If AI use among buyers keeps climbing from 19%, dealers will be negotiating with buyers who've already checked every number.
2. **The online financing gap.** Cox found buyers' preference for applying for financing online runs 15 points ahead of how often they actually do it, and 24 points ahead for choosing add-on products online. The dealer's finance office is where the most money hides, and it's the step that's moved online the least.
3. **Doc fees by state.** New York caps dealer documentation fees at $175. Listings near me in Maryland show processing charges of $800, labeled "not required by law." On the same car, that's a $625 difference before anyone negotiates.
4. **Days on the lot.** The car I bought had sat for about ten weeks. The one I passed on had been cut repeatedly over two months. Listing-history tools now show every price change. A long, steady markdown history is the seller telling you they're ready to talk.
5. **History-report detail.** Watch whether damage records get more specific. A one-line "damage reported" leaves the buyer guessing. The more detailed the record, the less a dealer can lean on a guess.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week — Showroom Edition *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"When dealers deliver intelligent, seamless experiences, it works."* — Lori Wittman, president of Retail Solutions, Cox Automotive, on the 2025 Car Buyer Journey study (Jan. 19, 2026).

**Translation:** "When buying a car stops being miserable, people like it."

**What it's hiding:** Nothing dishonest. It's true, and the satisfaction numbers back it up. But "seamless" describes how a purchase feels, not what it costs. The friction a smooth process removes is often the friction that protected the buyer: the pause to read the back page, the question about the damage, the second look at the fee lines. A seamless experience is great when the seller is transparent. When they aren't, it just makes it easier to sign. The seams are where you check the work.
