# How I'm Going to Test the Scourge of Sameness (and What Would Prove Me Wrong)

## Intro *(free)*

In #48, a client asked for income, no fees, and no stocks, and got a stock-sensitive fund with an annual fee. I called it the Scourge of Sameness: specific words go in, the shelf answer comes out.

One story is an anecdote. Two is a trend if you're on cable news. I want a dataset.

So this issue is a plan, published before I collect a single result: what I think is true, how I'm going to test it, and exactly what would make me drop it. If I write the rules after I see the data, I'm just another guy with a thesis and a ring light. Writing them first is the whole point.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Advisors are heavily regulated. Compliance is there to protect you."

Half true. Compliance is real, and it catches real misconduct. But it's worth separating two things that usually get blended together:

From the inside, it looked different to me. In my experience, compliance exists first to protect the firm: from regulators, lawsuits, and headlines. Most of the time, protecting the firm and protecting the client are the same thing. When they aren't, the process is built around the firm.

- **The rules.** Best-interest and fiduciary standards say the advice should serve the client.
- **The architecture.** Each firm decides what's on the approved product menu, how reps are paid, which products carry revenue-sharing, and what supervision flags as unusual.

Compliance enforces the rules *inside* the architecture. In my experience, that means an off-menu recommendation is harder for a rep to justify than an on-menu one, even when the off-menu answer fits the client better. That's my view from inside three firms. It's also a claim, and a claim without a test is just a podcast.

> **The Signal:** the question isn't whether advisors are good people. It's whether the structure they work in pulls different clients toward the same answer. That's measurable. So I'm going to measure it.

## The Number *(free)*

**142 of 284.** That's how many advisor visits ended with a recommendation for actively managed funds, in the best-known test of this question. Index funds came up in 21.

In 2008, economists Sendhil Mullainathan, Markus Nöth, and Antoinette Schoar sent trained mock clients to advisors in the Boston area, 284 visits from April to August. Some auditors showed up holding a diversified, low-fee index portfolio. Advisors were *more* likely to push those clients toward active funds. About 30% wouldn't give specific advice until the client moved money over. ([NBER working paper 17929](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17929), March 2012; [full paper](https://hub.hslu.ch/retailbanking/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2012/09/the_market_for_financial_advice_an_audit_study.pdf))

What would change its meaning, and why I'm running my own version: the sample was mostly commission-paid advisors at banks and retail firms. Independent, hourly, and fee-only advisors were mostly left out. Michael Kitces argued the study measured salespeople, not advisors ([Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/how-the-advisor-sting-study-completely-missed-the-mark/)). He has a point, and I'd rather fix it than argue with it. Without fee-only advisors in the sample, you can't tell whether the shelf answer comes from the *structure* or from the *profession*. That's the gap my test is built to close.

*Historical study; 2008 conditions. Not a forecast of any advisor's behavior.*

## The Read — the test, in advance *(paid)*

### The hypothesis

**Where advisors are paid by transactions and work from a firm product menu, recommendations converge on that menu regardless of what the client says they want. Where advisors are paid only by the client, recommendations track the client's stated goals more closely.**

If that's true, the Scourge of Sameness is structural. If it's false, it's either the profession or me, and I'll say which.

### The design: three channels, one script

| Channel | How they're paid | Standard | Target meetings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broker-dealer rep at a large firm | Commissions, product revenue | Reg BI (best interest) | 6 |
| Dual-registered advisor | Mix: fees and commissions | Switches by account type | 6 |
| Fee-only or hourly RIA | Client fees only | Fiduciary | 6 |

The fee-only channel is the control the 2008 study mostly lacked. If those advisors converge on the same kinds of answers, the structure isn't the cause.

**The scenario is real: mine.** I hold a concentrated, restricted pre-IPO position, and I'm weighing whether to sell when I'm allowed or wait. I'm a business owner. Nothing about me is invented. I'll say up front that I'm evaluating approaches and haven't decided to hire anyone, which is true.

**The script.** Same opening in every meeting: my situation, my goals (keep control of the decision, minimize ongoing fees, manage taxes on a low-basis position, no products I don't understand), and the same four questions:

1. What would you recommend, and why?
2. What does that cost me, in dollars, first year and ongoing?
3. What alternatives did you consider, including doing nothing?
4. How are you paid if I say yes?

### The scoring sheet

I fill this in within an hour of each meeting, from notes on a fixed form. Each item scores 0, 1, or 2.

| Item | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery | Recommended before asking | Asked a little | Asked before recommending |
| Goal match | Ignores stated goals | Partly matches | Maps to each stated goal |
| Cost in dollars | Not stated | Percent only | Dollars, first year and ongoing |
| Alternatives | None offered | Shelf alternatives only | Includes off-shelf or "do nothing" |
| Pay disclosure | Dodged | Vague | Clear, unprompted or on first ask |
| Off-shelf | Only firm or partner products | Mixed | Off-shelf where it fits |

I also log one flag per meeting: **shelf convergence**, meaning the core recommendation was a managed account, packaged product, or proprietary fund, rather than advice on what I already own.

**Bias control.** I'm scoring my own meetings, and I have a bias roughly the size of this newsletter. So after each meeting, I'll strip the notes of anything that reveals the channel and have a second person score them blind. Where we disagree, both scores get published.

### What would change my mind (committed now)

With 18 meetings, this is directional evidence, not proof. So I'm setting thresholds in advance:

- **Thesis supported:** the fee-only channel's average Goal match beats the broker-dealer channel by at least 0.5 points, *and* shelf convergence in the broker-dealer channel is at least double the fee-only rate.
- **Thesis dropped:** fee-only advisors converge on packaged products at a similar rate to broker-dealer reps, *or* the Goal match gap is under 0.25 points.
- **Inconclusive:** anything in between. I'll publish it as inconclusive, not round it in my favor.

One more honest risk: my scenario is unusual. A restricted pre-IPO position may pull every channel toward specialized answers, which could hide the effect. That's why there's a second arm.

### The reader arm

I'll open a submission form for readers who've had an advisor meeting: what you told them you wanted, what they recommended, what it cost, how they're paid, and which channel they're in. Same rubric. Real clients, real meetings, and a sample I can't steer, which is the point.

### The rules I'm holding myself to

- My real situation only. No invented assets or goals.
- No recording without everyone's consent. Notes only by default.
- No advisor or firm named. Results are reported by channel.
- Every result gets published, including the ones that hurt my thesis.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction. Whether or not you ever join the reader arm, these are the tells I'd watch for in any advisor meeting:

1. **The order of operations.** Did they ask about you before they talked about products? A recommendation that arrives before the questions is the shelf talking.
2. **Percent versus dollars.** Fees quoted only in percentages are easy to wave off. Ask for the number in dollars, first year and every year after.
3. **"Move it here first."** In the 2008 study, about 30% of advisors held back specific advice until money moved. Advice that depends on a transfer is a sales step.
4. **Whether "do nothing" is on the menu.** A good advisor will tell you when what you already own is fine. Notice whether it ever comes up.
5. **The hat.** If they're dual-registered, ask which standard applies to the specific account they're recommending. The answer can change mid-meeting.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"this fund has 2% fee but that is not very much above industry average"* — an unnamed advisor, as recorded in the Mullainathan, Nöth, and Schoar audit study ([paper](https://hub.hslu.ch/retailbanking/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2012/09/the_market_for_financial_advice_an_audit_study.pdf), 2008 field visits). *(Verify verbatim against the paper before publishing.)*

**Translation:** "It's expensive, but so are the others I could sell you."

**What it's hiding:** "Industry average" is an average of other people's fees, not a measure of value. Comparing a 2% fund to an expensive peer group makes it sound normal. Comparing it to a low-cost index fund owning similar assets makes it sound like what it is. The anchor you're handed decides whether the fee looks small. In the study, this kind of line came up when fees came up at all: fees were mentioned in 160 of 281 visits, often to wave them off.
