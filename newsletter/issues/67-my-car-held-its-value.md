# My Car Held 71% of Its Value. That's Not Good News.

## Intro *(free)*

I grew up with a simple rule about cars: a $35,000 car is a $5,000 car ten years later. Nobody taught it to me. It was just true, the way gravity is true.

Then a delivery truck totaled my car this fall, and the insurer's valuation broke the rule. I got the car new in 2019, with an MSRP of $26,895. About seven years and 54,000 miles later, the insurer valued it at $19,042 before tax and fees.

That's 71% of what it cost new. My first reaction was pride. My second reaction, after I ran the numbers, was less flattering. This issue is the second reaction.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Buy a good car, keep it up, and it'll hold its value."

It sounds like the reward for doing everything right. Buy a reliable brand, keep the receipts, change the oil on time, and the market pays you back. My car is Exhibit A: 71 cents on the dollar after seven years.

Here's the math it leaves out. The $19,042 is in 2026 dollars. The $26,895 was in 2019 dollars. Those aren't the same dollars.

Consumer prices rose about 32% between the 2019 average and September 2026 (CPI-U, 255.657 to 336.940, Bureau of Labor Statistics). Put the valuation back into 2019 money and it's worth about **$14,450**. That's **54%** of the sticker price, not 71%.

Run it the other direction and it gets funnier. My $26,895 car would cost about **$35,450** in today's money. The "$35,000 car" from my childhood rule is my car. I just got mine before the price tag caught up.

So the car lost close to half its real value in seven years. That's still a decent result. It's just not a miracle, and most of the "holding" was done by the dollar shrinking underneath it.

> **The Signal:** your car didn't hold its value. Your money lost some of its value, and the car's price tag moved with it.

## The Number *(free)*

**+46%.** That's how much wholesale used-vehicle prices rose from December 2019 to September 2026, as measured by the Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index (141.1 to 205.9, Cox Automotive).

Over the same stretch, consumer prices rose 31% (CPI-U, 256.974 to 336.940). New-vehicle average transaction prices rose 30%, from $38,948 to $50,562 (Kelley Blue Book).

So used cars didn't just keep pace with inflation. They outran it by about 15 percentage points. Part of my car's "held value" was inflation. The rest was a used-car market that got more expensive than everything else.

What would change its meaning: the index measures wholesale auction prices, adjusted for mix and mileage, not what you pay at retail. And it's moving. September was down 0.6% from a year earlier, and Cox's chief economist described depreciation as "steeper than we typically see this time of year." If the gap closes, the 15-point premium is the part that disappears first.

## The Read — strong resale is a replacement-cost problem *(paid)*

**Thesis:** a car "holding its value" only makes you richer if you're leaving the car market. If you need another car, and nearly everyone who owns one does, the same price level that inflated your car's value inflates the cost of the next one. In my view, for most owners it's a paper gain they can't spend.

### The mechanism

Four forces stacked on top of each other.

**1. The dollar.** Roughly 31% inflation since late 2019. Every price got bigger, including the one on your car. That alone explains most of my 71%.

**2. A missing generation of cars.** The 2021–2022 chip shortage cut new-vehicle production, and buyers who couldn't get a new car bought used. Fewer new cars sold then means fewer three- and four-year-old cars available now. Industry analysts have pointed to this thin cohort as one reason used prices stayed high after the shortage ended.

**3. New cars moved out of reach.** The average new vehicle now transacts at $50,562. When the new-car floor rises that far, buyers move down to used, and the good used cars get bid up.

**4. Durability.** A well-kept car with 100,000 miles is no longer near the end of its life. Buyers price in the miles left, not the miles used, and that keeps older cars expensive.

### Why it's a costume, not wealth

Here's my own replacement math, kept general. The insurance check, after paying off the remaining loan, is enough for a solid down payment and not much more. A comparable or slightly newer car in the same class costs more than my car was valued at, because every car in the class rode the same wave. I'm not trading up with my windfall. I'm trading sideways and financing the gap.

It's the same logic as your house. If every house on your street went up 40%, you're richer only if you're willing to stop owning a house, or move somewhere cheaper. Otherwise the gain is a number on a statement, and the next house costs 40% more too.

The people who actually capture the gain are the ones leaving the category: someone selling a second car, an estate liquidating a parent's car, or a household going from two cars to one.

### The fair case for the other side

High resale values aren't all costume. They have real benefits:

- **Lower cost of ownership.** If you sell eventually, depreciation is the biggest cost of owning a car, and slow depreciation cuts it. Owners who sell into a strong market keep real money.
- **Bigger total-loss checks.** My insurer paid what replacing the car costs, not what my old rule said. That's the system working.
- **Better collateral.** Borrowers whose cars hold value are less likely to owe more than the car is worth.

Those benefits are real. They just mostly go to people at the exits: sellers, claimants, and lenders. The owner who needs to keep driving gets them on paper.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **Used prices fall back to the inflation line.** Take the December 2019 index of 141.1 and grow it at CPI's pace, and you get about **185**. September's reading was 205.9, roughly 11% above that line. If the index falls toward 185 and new-car prices hold, the replacement gap shrinks, and today's sellers will look smart for selling.
2. **New-car prices fall in real terms.** If average transaction prices flatten while inflation continues, the down-market push weakens, and the used premium should ease with it.
3. **Older cars stop commanding a durability premium.** If high-mileage cars start depreciating like they used to, the "miles left" pricing breaks, and my 71% becomes a one-time accident of timing.

The honest bottom line: this is one car, one valuation, one point in time. But the index data says my experience wasn't unusual, and the mechanism doesn't depend on my car being special.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction:

1. **The Manheim index against the ~185 line.** It's the cleanest single gauge of whether the post-2020 premium is unwinding. Cox publishes a mid-month reading and a full-month reading.
2. **New-vehicle transaction prices.** At $50,562, they're $52 below the record set in December 2025. If they keep climbing, the pressure on used prices continues.
3. **Off-lease supply.** Fewer leases were written during the shortage years, which means fewer cars coming off lease a few years later. When that supply recovers, used prices should feel it.
4. **Auto loan rates.** A cheaper car at a higher rate can cost more per month than a pricier car at a lower one. The monthly payment is where most households actually feel this.
5. **Insurance premiums.** Higher vehicle values mean higher replacement costs on every claim. The check I'm describing gets priced back into everyone's premium.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week — Used Car Edition *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"Many of the metrics we routinely track are converging back toward pre-pandemic norms."* — Jeremy Robb, chief economist, Cox Automotive, Q3 2026 Manheim index release (Oct. 7, 2026).

**Translation:** "The way prices move is starting to look like 2019 again."

**What it's hiding:** Nothing dishonest. It's a careful economist's sentence, and it's accurate. But "converging toward pre-pandemic norms" describes behavior: seasonality, depreciation pace, supply metrics. It doesn't describe the price level, which is still about 46% above December 2019. A reader can hear "back to normal" and picture 2019 prices. The norms are coming back. The prices aren't.
