# Cars Last Longer Than Ever. So Why Does Every Repair Feel Like a Ransom Note?

## Intro *(free)*

I've spent the last few weeks shopping for a family car. It's taught me more about the car business than seven years of owning one.

Here's what stood out. The cars in my price range aren't worn out at 50,000 miles. Most of them will run past 200,000. What scared me wasn't whether they'd last. It was what each one would cost to fix when something broke.

A turbocharger. A ten-speed transmission. A camera behind the windshield that needs recalibrating every time the glass gets replaced. My last car had none of those. Most of the cars I looked at have all three.

So this issue is about a gap: cars last longer than they ever have, and keeping them running costs more than it ever has. Both are true at the same time.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "They don't build them like they used to."

You've heard it at every barbecue. Somebody's uncle had a truck that ran forever, and the new stuff is junk.

The data says the opposite. The average car and light truck on U.S. roads is **12.8 years old**, a record (S&P Global Mobility, May 2025). For passenger cars alone, it's 14.5 years. People don't keep junk for 14 years. They keep cars that still work.

What the uncle is actually feeling is the bill. Motor vehicle maintenance and repair prices rose **5.8%** in the 12 months through September, against 3.7% for consumer prices overall (Bureau of Labor Statistics, Oct. 14, 2026). The car didn't get worse. Fixing it got more expensive.

> **The Signal:** they build them to last longer. They also build them to cost more to fix.

## The Number *(free)*

**12.8 years.** That's the average age of a light vehicle on U.S. roads, the highest S&P Global Mobility has recorded. It rose two months from 12.6 years in the prior year's reading, which was itself a record.

Two things push that number up. Cars last longer. And new cars cost so much that people hold on to the one they have. Both mean more old cars on the road, and more old cars need more repairs.

What would change its meaning: average age is a fleet number, not a lifespan. It rises when people buy fewer new cars, even if no car lasts a day longer. A year of strong new-car sales would pull it down without anything changing in the engineering.

## The Read — durability and repairability split apart *(paid)*

**Thesis:** for most of the last century, a car that lasted longer was also a car that was cheaper to keep running. Those two things have split. In my view, modern cars are built to last and built to be expensive to fix, and the second part is where the industry's money increasingly comes from.

### The mechanism

Four engineering choices stacked on top of each other. Each one made sense on its own.

**1. Smaller engines with turbochargers and direct injection.** Fuel-economy rules pushed automakers toward small turbocharged engines that inject fuel straight into the cylinder. They're efficient and they make good power. They also add parts that wear: the turbo itself, a high-pressure fuel pump, and intake valves that no longer get washed by fuel and can build up carbon over time. Some owners end up paying for a carbon cleaning a port-injected engine never needed.

**2. More gears.** Eight, nine and ten-speed automatics, continuously variable transmissions, and dual-clutch units squeeze out fuel economy too. When they work, you never notice them. When they don't, a transmission that used to be rebuilt by a local shop is often replaced as a unit.

**3. Sensors everywhere.** Cameras, radar and ultrasonic sensors run the safety systems: automatic braking, lane-keeping, blind-spot alerts. Those systems prevent crashes, and I want them in a car my kids ride in. But they live in the bumpers, mirrors and windshield, the parts that get hit. AAA put the added cost of repairing these systems at $500 to $1,300 for a front radar sensor, $300 to $1,000 for ultrasonic parking sensors, and about $360 just to recalibrate a windshield camera (AAA, December 2023). A fender bender used to be a body-shop job. Now it's a body-shop job plus an electronics job.

**4. Software.** Modern cars run on dozens of computer modules, and replacement parts often have to be coded to the car before they work. That routes more repairs through dealers and through shops that pay for the right tools and data. Cox Automotive's chief economist has pointed out that upkeep and repair costs have been a big part of transportation's contribution to inflation, partly because repairs now take more technician time and skill (as reported by Autobody News, September 2026).

None of these four is a conspiracy. Each one solved a real problem: fuel economy, emissions, safety. The cost of all four landed in the same place, which is the repair bill.

### Who the bill is good for

Here's the part that isn't on the window sticker. A car that lasts 14 years and costs more to fix every year is a revenue stream, not just a product.

The dealer service department is usually one of the steadiest profit centers a dealership has. Parts suppliers sell more expensive parts. And automakers are now selling services that keep paying after the sale. GM told investors it expected deferred revenue, much of it from software and services like OnStar and its Super Cruise driver-assist system, to reach $7.5 billion in 2026, up from $5.4 billion in 2025 (GM fourth-quarter 2025 earnings call, Jan. 27, 2026).

I'm not saying anyone designed cars to break. I'm saying the industry's incentives no longer reward making cars cheap to fix, and a business tends to get more of what it's paid for.

### The fair case for the other side

Modern cars deserve real credit:

- **They last.** A 14-year average age for passenger cars is a durability record, not a scandal. Hitting 100,000 miles used to mean a car was near the end. Now it's closer to the middle.
- **They protect people.** Automatic emergency braking and the sensors behind it prevent crashes. A $360 calibration is cheap next to an injury.
- **Routine maintenance got cheaper per mile.** Synthetic oil, long-life spark plugs and longer service intervals mean fewer trips to the shop for the basics.
- **Part of the increase is labor.** Technicians are scarce, and every service business has been raising wages. Some of the 5.8% is just what skilled labor costs now.

All of that is true. It just means the cost moved. You visit the shop less often, and each visit costs more.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **Repair inflation falls back to headline.** If the maintenance-and-repair index runs at or below overall CPI for a full year, the "ransom note" was a post-pandemic labor and parts shock, not a structural shift.
2. **Average age flattens while new-car sales are steady.** If people stop holding cars longer even though they can afford to replace them, the durability side of my story is weaker than I think.
3. **Independent shops win access.** If right-to-repair efforts give independent shops the same diagnostic data, tools and parts coding as dealers, the software premium should shrink, and I'd expect repair prices to show it.

The honest bottom line: I'm one buyer in one market, and my list of worries is shaped by the cars I looked at. But the price data and the fleet data point the same way, and the mechanism doesn't depend on any one brand.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction:

1. **CPI maintenance and repair against headline CPI.** It's the cleanest monthly read on whether the gap is closing. BLS also splits out maintenance and servicing (up 7.9% over the year through August) from repair (up 2.6%), which shows where the pressure sits.
2. **S&P Global Mobility's next average-age reading.** It usually lands in late spring. Another record says owners are still choosing repair over replacement.
3. **Auto insurance.** The insurance index fell 5.0% over the year through September, but that follows a run that left it about 50% above August 2021 levels. Repair costs feed straight into premiums, so this is where the ransom note shows up for people who never get in a crash.
4. **Automaker services revenue.** Watch how often deferred revenue, subscriptions and "software-defined vehicles" come up on earnings calls. The more the industry talks about it, the more the car is a platform for after-sale billing.
5. **Your own cost per mile.** If you're shopping, price the expensive parts before you buy: the transmission type, whether the engine is turbocharged, and what a windshield replacement with calibration costs at a local shop.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week — Aftermarket Edition *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"With average age growth, more vehicles are entering the prime range for aftermarket service."* — Todd Campau, aftermarket practice lead, S&P Global Mobility, annual vehicle-age analysis (May 2024).

**Translation:** "Your car is old enough to start paying us."

**What it's hiding:** Nothing dishonest. It's written for the parts and service industry, and to that audience it's simply good news. But read it from the driver's seat. "Prime range for aftermarket service" is the stretch of years when your car stops being a purchase and starts being a stream of bills. The same fact is a headwind for the owner and a tailwind for everyone who fixes the car, and the sentence only describes one side.
