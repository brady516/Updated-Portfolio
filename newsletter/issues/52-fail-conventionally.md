# Fail Conventionally: The Career Risk of Being Right Alone

## Intro *(free)*

In my Morgan Stanley office, I was 1 of 42 advisors running a practice built on individual stocks and bonds. At Merrill, 1 of 38. At Edward Jones, one of only two I knew of on the entire East Coast. The other was in South Carolina.

That's not a brag. It's a data point. When 41 out of 42 professionals do the same thing, it's tempting to assume they all reached the same conclusion independently. Sometimes they did. Sometimes they all read the same incentive.

John Maynard Keynes put it in one sentence in 1936: "Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally." Ninety years later, in my experience, that sentence still runs a lot of the industry. This issue is about why, and about what it costs the person on the other side of the desk.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Every major firm agrees. The consensus can't be that wrong."

Consensus can be right. It often is. But ask what happens to the person who breaks from it, and you'll see why agreement is cheaper to produce than accuracy.

The payoffs aren't symmetric. Be wrong with the crowd, and you were unlucky; so was everyone. Be wrong alone, and you were reckless, and your name is on it. Be right alone, and you get a pat on the back, maybe. Be right with the crowd, and you get paid like everyone else. If you're a professional whose job depends on not standing out for the wrong reason, the rational move is to stand close to everyone else.

That's not a character flaw. It's a payoff table. And it means some of the agreement you see isn't independent judgment at all. It's career insurance.

> **The Signal:** when everyone agrees, ask two questions. Did they each get there on their own? And what would it cost any one of them to disagree out loud?

## The Number *(free)*

**1.42% to 1.83% a year.** That's how much "closet indexers" underperformed their benchmarks, net of fees, in Martijn Cremers and Antti Petajisto's 2009 study of active mutual funds from 1990 to 2003. As summarized by [Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/using-active-share-to-avoid-closet-indexer-investment-management-fees/).

A closet indexer is a fund that charges for active management while holding something close to the index. It's the purest form of failing conventionally: stay near the benchmark so you can never be badly wrong alone, and collect an active fee for the privilege. The funds that actually deviated the most did better in that study, outperforming by about 1.13% to 1.15% a year.

What would change its meaning: the finding is disputed. Researchers at AQR re-ran the data with different benchmark adjustments and found high active share wasn't a reliable predictor of outperformance, and part of the original edge came from benchmark effects in that period. The part nobody really disputes is the cheap version: paying active fees for index-like exposure is a reliable way to trail the index by roughly the fee.

*Historical study, 1990–2003. Not a forecast for any fund.*

## The Read — the payoff table behind the advice *(paid)*

**Thesis:** professionals inside large firms face a lopsided payoff for disagreeing with the consensus. That pushes their advice toward the middle whether or not the middle fits you. The fix isn't to distrust every professional. It's to read the incentive before you read the recommendation.

### The payoff table

|  | With the crowd | Alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Right** | Paid like everyone else | Maybe a pat on the back |
| **Wrong** | "Nobody saw it coming" | Your name, your problem |

The bottom-right cell is the one that ends careers. The top-right cell rarely makes them. So the expected value of going it alone is worse for the professional, even when it's better for the client. Nobody ever got fired for recommending the model portfolio.

### The evidence it changes behavior

This isn't just a theory. Harrison Hong, Jeffrey Kubik, and Amit Solomon studied sell-side analysts and found that inexperienced analysts were more likely to be terminated for inaccurate forecasts than experienced ones. More telling: controlling for accuracy, they were also more likely to be terminated for **bold forecasts that deviated from the consensus**. And the inexperienced analysts responded the way the incentive says they should. They deviated less ([RAND Journal of Economics, 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v31y2000ispringp121-144.html)).

Read that again. An analyst could be *equally accurate* and still be punished more for being different. When being different costs you even when you're right, you learn not to be different.

### The same math, inside an advice firm

Now move it from research to the advisor's desk. In my experience, the incentives at large firms point the same way:

- **Model portfolios and approved lists** are the consensus, built into the software. Recommending them needs no defense. Going off them does.
- **Production targets** measure revenue, and the products that generate it are easy to sell and easy to defend.
- **Supervision** is built to flag the unusual. An individual stock-and-bond portfolio is, by definition, unusual.

None of this requires a bad advisor. It requires a normal person reading a normal payoff table. That's how you get 41 of 42 people in one office doing the same thing. In my view, firm management disfavored stock-and-bond portfolios, and the structure made it easier to be one of the 41 than the one.

*(That's my experience and opinion from three firms, not a finding about any firm's policy.)*

### Why that matters to you

The consensus answer isn't automatically wrong for you. It might be exactly right. The problem is that you can't tell from the outside whether you got it because it fits you or because it was the safest thing for the professional to say. A recommendation that protects the advisor and one that serves you can look identical across the desk.

That's the point of #48 and the test in #50: check the recommendation against your own words. If it matches, great. If it matches the shelf instead, you've found the incentive.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **Contrarian professionals get rewarded, not punished.** If firms promote and pay advisors and analysts for well-reasoned deviations, the payoff table I drew is wrong.
2. **Consensus advice performs as well for clients as tailored advice.** If the shelf answer fits most people about as well as a custom one, the cost of herding is small, and this is a philosophical complaint, not a financial one.
3. **Independent advisors herd just as much.** If fee-only advisors with no product incentive converge on the same answers, the cause is the profession, not the corporate structure. (That's exactly what #50 is built to test.)

If the evidence lands there, I'll write the issue that says so.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction. A few tells that you might be getting the safe answer instead of the right one:

1. **"Everyone's recommending this."** Popularity is a defense, not a reason. Ask for the reason.
2. **Hedged language with no number attached.** "Well positioned," "appropriately diversified," "in line with our house view." These are sentences built to survive a bad outcome, not to predict a good one.
3. **The firm's view, quoted in place of the advisor's.** Ask what *they* think. If the answer is always the house view, you're talking to the house.
4. **No downside named.** An advisor who's thought hard about a recommendation can tell you how it fails. An advisor protecting their career would rather not put that on the record.
5. **Your situation is unusual and the answer isn't.** If your goals are specific and the recommendation is generic, the incentive won.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"Our recommendation is consistent with the firm's house view and appropriate for your risk profile."* *(Illustrative archetype. Swap for a sourced, verbatim quote before publishing.)*

**Translation:** "If this goes wrong, it won't be my fault."

**What it's hiding:** Every word in that sentence is about defensibility, not about you. "Consistent with the house view" means the decision was made upstairs. "Appropriate for your risk profile" means it fits a category you were sorted into, not necessarily the person you are. It's the sentence you'd write if the goal were to survive the review, not to win the outcome. Fail conventionally, in writing.
