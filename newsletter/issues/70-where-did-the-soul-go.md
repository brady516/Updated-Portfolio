# Where Did the Soul Go? A Business-School Case Study Starring Me

## Cold Open *(free)*

The salesman handed me the keys to a brand-new family sedan and said, "Sometimes you just have to make the responsible decision."

He sells this car for a living. That's like a bartender telling you to try the water.

I merged onto the highway and put my foot all the way down. Nothing happened, then something happened slowly. The engine was a 1.5-liter turbo four, and it made the sound a blender makes when you ask too much of it. Cloth seats. A plastic steering wheel. A dashboard that looked like it had been designed by a committee that had never been in a hurry.

"Next exit," I said. "We're going back."

We drove the hybrid version next. It was nicer. It had a leather-wrapped steering wheel and seats made of a material the brochure calls "leatherette," which is a word that means "not leather." In sport mode, under full throttle, it sounded like a remote-control car that had learned to be sad.

Here's the thing. It's a good car. It's efficient, it's safe, it will run forever, and millions of people will be happy with it. It just doesn't have a soul. And somewhere between the second test drive and the drive home, I stopped asking which car to buy and started asking a different question.

Where did the soul go? And who took it?

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Nobody wants big engines anymore. The market moved on."

It's the explanation you hear most, and it's tidy. Buyers wanted fuel economy, automakers listened, the V6 retired with honors.

Except the market didn't move on everywhere. Full-size pickups kept their V8s. Performance trucks got bigger engines, not smaller ones. The engines that disappeared were mostly in one place: the affordable family sedan.

That pattern has a cause. Federal fuel-economy rules, known as CAFE, set a sales-weighted average each automaker has to hit across its fleet, and since 2011 the target for each vehicle has depended on its size. A full-size pickup gets an easier target than a midsize sedan. So the cheapest place to find fuel economy was the family car, and that's where the engines shrank. The rule didn't ban the V6. It just made it expensive to keep in the cars most of us actually buy.

> **The Signal:** the market didn't kill the family-sedan V6 on its own. A rule decided where the efficiency had to come from, and the family sedan drew the short straw.

## The Number *(free)*

**$0.** That's the civil penalty for missing a federal fuel-economy standard, as of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025. The standards still exist on paper and automakers still report against them. Missing them just doesn't cost anything anymore.

Five months later, on Dec. 3, 2025, the Department of Transportation proposed cutting the 2031 fleet target from 50.4 miles per gallon to 34.5. That proposal isn't final.

So the rule I'm blaming for the missing soul has lost its teeth. That gives us something rare in this newsletter: a clean experiment. If the rule was the cause, engines with character should start coming back to family cars within a model cycle or two. If they don't, the market and global engine programs did more of the killing than the rule did.

What would change its meaning: other emissions rules, state standards and the cost of re-engineering an engine all still push toward small engines. A $0 penalty removes one reason, not every reason. And a future Congress can put the penalty back.

## The Read — The Mad Finantist and the Search for Soul *(paid)*

**A case study in entrepreneurship, consumer demand and regulatory change.**

In October 2026, a delivery truck totaled the car the protagonist had owned since new. It was two payments from paid off and was meant to become his drag car. The insurance check paid market value. It didn't pay for what the car meant to him, and the replacement market made that gap obvious. Used prices sat well above 2019 levels. The trucks he trusted carried a steep premium for their reputation. And a test drive of a new family sedan ended at the next exit.

"It's not that they're plastic," he said. "It's not that they're all turbos. They lost their soul."

**Discussion question 1: who took it?**

Both suspects are guilty. The rule went first. Size-based fuel-economy targets made the family sedan the cheapest place to find efficiency, and small turbocharged engines were the cheapest way to pass the official tests. The market finished the job. Buyers moved to crossovers, most of them never press the gas pedal to the floor, and nobody wrote a letter when the V6 left.

**Discussion question 2: can you make money selling soul?**

Some evidence says yes. The Dodge Charger sold its V8 sedan for nearly two decades and built a following that outlived the car. Some evidence says no. The Chevrolet SS was close to exactly what I want: a family sedan with a V8 and an available manual transmission. It ran four model years, from 2014 to 2017, sold in small numbers, and was quietly dropped. A cult following is not the same thing as volume.

**Discussion question 3: what should he build?**

There are three options, and the case only works if you're honest about which one you can afford.

1. **A manufacturer.** Design a car, crash-test it, certify its emissions, build a factory, fund a warranty reserve, and find dealers. Before the first sale, that's hundreds of millions of dollars at minimum. Well-funded startups have gone bankrupt at this step.
2. **An upfitter.** Start with a car that's already certified and build the soul in afterward: a supercharger, a proper transmission, suspension done right. Smaller capital, real margins, and a customer who chooses exactly how much soul to pay for.
3. **Neither.** Buy a well-built sedan with a naturally aspirated V6 while they still exist, and race something else on weekends. Unglamorous, but the math works on the first day.

**The startup that did the opposite.** Slate Auto, backed by Jeff Bezos and Thomas Tull's TWG Global, raised a $650 million Series C in April 2026 to build a stripped-down electric pickup in the mid-$20,000s. It uses about 700 parts, less than half the usual count. Its factory is a converted printing plant in Warsaw, Indiana. There's no paint shop: color comes from vinyl wraps applied at a separate facility. The base truck doesn't even have power windows. More than 160,000 people have put down refundable deposits.

Slate's lesson isn't that electric beats gas. It's that the only way a car startup survives is by being ruthlessly simple and backed by money that can wait. Slate sells the simplest possible truck and lets buyers add what they want. A soul brand built the same way would sell a simple, certified platform and offer the soul as the upgrade. That's option 2 with better marketing.

### The fair case for the efficient cars

I've been rude to the family sedan, so here's what it deserves:

- **It's genuinely good engineering.** Small turbo engines and hybrids make more power and use less fuel than the engines they replaced. My old S-10 got about 10 miles per gallon on a good day.
- **It's safer and cleaner.** Modern family cars protect occupants and pollute far less than the cars I'm nostalgic for.
- **The rollback has real supporters and real critics.** The White House says relaxing the standards will save families money, claiming the prior rules would have raised the average cost of a new car by nearly $1,000. Critics argue drivers will pay more at the pump and the air will be dirtier. Both sides have numbers, and readers should weigh them.
- **Most buyers don't want what I want.** "Soul" is a preference, not a defect. The market is allowed to disagree with me.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **No soul comes back.** If, with the penalty at $0, no major automaker brings a naturally aspirated six or eight back to an affordable family sedan by the 2029 model year, the rule wasn't the main cause.
2. **The soul cars don't sell.** If the ones that do come back sell like the Chevy SS, small and briefly, then the demand I'm describing is mostly people like me, and there aren't enough of us.
3. **The upfit market stays tiny.** If tuning shops that sell turnkey builds on certified platforms can't grow beyond a few hundred cars a year, option 2 is a hobby, not a business.

The honest bottom line: I don't know yet whether soul is a market or a mood. But for the first time in 15 years, the rule that shaped every family sedan has been switched off, and we get to watch what automakers do without it.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction:

1. **Engine announcements for model years 2028 and 2029.** Any affordable sedan that adds a larger, naturally aspirated engine is the first data point for the experiment.
2. **The final CAFE rule.** The Dec. 3, 2025 proposal still has to become final, and it may be challenged in court. The final target number matters more than the proposal.
3. **Slate's first deliveries.** Deliveries are targeted for late 2026. Raising money isn't the hard part of a car startup. Building cars profitably is.
4. **The credit market.** Automakers that beat the standards used to sell credits to the ones that didn't. With penalties at $0, those credits are worth much less. Watch which companies lose that revenue and which stop needing it.
5. **Upfitter and tuner growth.** If small builders selling finished performance versions of existing cars start raising outside capital, investors are betting that soul is a market.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week — Factory Edition *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"Slate has remained laser-focused on the steps needed to develop our vehicle and reindustrialize our Warsaw factory."* — Chris Barman, president of vehicles, Slate Auto, on the company's $650 million raise (reported by IndustryWeek, April 14, 2026).

**Translation:** "We're turning an old printing plant into a truck factory, and it's going fine."

**What it's hiding:** Nothing dishonest. It's a careful, accurate sentence. But "reindustrialize" does a lot of work. It turns a factory renovation into a national project, and it sounds like progress before a single truck has been delivered. The steps are real. The finish line is still the first truck in a customer's driveway, and the first year of profitable ones after that.
