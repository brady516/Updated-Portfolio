# Your Backlog Is My Capex

## Intro *(free)*

This week, a retiree was told there's no recession coming until about 2028. The evidence: a memory-chip maker has its orders booked two years out.

That backlog is real. The conclusion is where it gets creative. Follow the money one step further. Every dollar in a chipmaker's backlog is a dollar a tech giant committed to spend. One company's revenue is another company's capital expenditure. And capex is a promise one CFO makes to a future CFO. It's a bet on earnings, not a record of them.

This issue is about the gap between those two things, and why I think there's hot air in the AI trade right now. I'll show you the numbers, the best case against me, and what would prove me wrong.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Memory makers are sold out for two years. Demand is booming. The economy is fine through 2028."

The facts in that sentence check out. Micron says supply will be tighter in 2027 and 2028 than in 2026, with more than 75% of its 2027 output already committed ([CIO](https://www.cio.com/article/4229625/memory-squeeze-set-to-tighten-through-2028-micron-says.html), 10/1/2026). SanDisk's multiyear contracts are expected to cover about two-thirds of the bits it sells in fiscal 2028 ([AOL](https://www.aol.com/articles/micron-says-memory-supply-much-035802000.html), 10/2/2026).

The conclusion doesn't. Ask who is placing those orders. Mostly a handful of hyperscalers building AI data centers. Their purchase is the chipmaker's revenue, but on their own books it's capex. It's money spent today on the assumption that AI products will earn it back later.

So the backlog proves one thing: a few very large companies are very willing to spend. Willingness to spend has never been the scarce resource in a boom. It doesn't prove the spending pays off. And it says nothing about the consumer, the job market, or a retiree's income.

> **The Signal:** a supplier's backlog measures the buyer's conviction, not the buyer's returns. To judge the AI trade, look at the buyers' AI revenue, not the sellers' order books.

## The Number *(free)*

**$230 billion a year.** That's the gap between what this year's AI buildout costs to run and what AI is earning so far, by Goldman Sachs' estimate.

As reported by [Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/328112/20260928/goldman-sachs-quantifies-ais-230b-annual-gap-800b-spent-300b-needed-break-even.htm) (9/28/2026), Goldman's September 25 note puts 2026 AI infrastructure spending by the five largest U.S. hyperscalers at about $800 billion. Covering the run-rate cost of that buildout would take about $300 billion a year in AI revenue. As of Q2 2026, AI revenue sat about $70 billion above the pre-AI trend line. $300 billion minus $70 billion is the $230 billion gap.

What would change the meaning: AI revenue compounding fast enough to close the gap within a few years. It's also an estimate built on depreciation assumptions, and those are the softest numbers in the whole debate (more below the line).

## The Read — capex isn't earnings *(paid)*

**Thesis:** the market is treating AI capital spending as if it were proof of AI earnings. It isn't. Capex is a bet, and accounting lets the bet look cheaper today than it will later. That gap between spending and payoff is where I think the hot air sits. This is my view, and below is exactly what would prove it wrong.

### Mechanism 1: capex hits earnings slowly, and the speed is a choice

When a company buys $100 of servers, it doesn't deduct $100 from this year's profit. It spreads the cost as depreciation over the equipment's "useful life." A longer assumed life means a smaller annual charge and higher reported earnings. Same servers, same cash out the door, better-looking quarter.

The big buyers have lengthened those lives:

| Company | Change | Disclosed | Stated effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alphabet | Servers 4 → 6 years | Feb 2023 | About $3.4B less depreciation in 2023 ([DCD](https://datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/google-increases-server-life-to-six-years-will-save-billions-of-dollars)) |
| Meta | Servers 4–5 → 5.5 years | Jan 2025 | $2.9B less depreciation in 2025, almost 4% of estimated pretax profit ([Accounting Today](https://accountingtoday.com/articles/metas-accounting-move-on-ai-servers-to-boost-profit)) |

Those changes can be legitimate. Servers do last longer than they used to, and older chips can be moved to lighter work. But AI chips are the fastest-improving hardware in the building. If they become uneconomic in three years, the earnings being reported today are partly borrowed from later years. Not stolen. Borrowed, with the bill dated later.

### Mechanism 2: the funding has shifted from cash to credit

For years, these companies paid for capex out of operating cash flow. That's changing. Per [FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/hyperscalers-tap-external-financing-as-ai-capex-outruns-cash-flow) (7/23/2026), covering Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle:

- Aggregate cash capex is expected to top $690 billion for fiscal 2026, up more than 80% year over year.
- Incremental debt went from 9% of capex in fiscal 2024 to 32% over the twelve months through June 2026.
- Free cash flow is expected near zero or negative for all but Alphabet and Microsoft.
- Alphabet priced an $84.75 billion equity raise in June 2026. S&P cut Oracle to BBB- in July.
- Off-balance-sheet lease commitments total about $820 billion.

UBS estimates Amazon, Alphabet, and Microsoft will spend about 102% of their cloud revenue on capex this year ([24/7 Wall St., via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/ai-absurd-spending-boom-hyperscalers-162709082.html), 8/22/2026). That's cloud revenue only, not their whole business, but it's the business the AI spending is supposed to feed.

### The bull case, in its strongest form

These aren't fragile companies. Total debt is about 1x EBITDA or below for four of the five, per FactSet. The demand looks real: AWS grew 36.7% to $42.2 billion in Q2, and Amazon raised its 2026 capex outlook to about $220 billion because it still can't meet demand ([Data Center Knowledge](https://datacenterknowledge.com/infrastructure/amazon-lifts-ai-infrastructure-spending-to-220b-as-demand-outpaces-capacity), 7/31/2026). Every major platform shift, from railroads to fiber to cloud, looked overbuilt before revenue caught up. A capex boom isn't a bubble if the revenue shows up.

That's a serious argument, and I'd rather lose to it than beat a strawman. My disagreement is about timing and proof. Capacity-constrained demand at today's prices tells you people want the product. It doesn't yet tell you the product earns back $800 billion a year of buildout.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **The gap closes.** AI revenue grows fast enough that Goldman-style break-even gaps shrink year over year, not widen.
2. **Cash flow catches up.** Debt's share of capex falls back toward single digits and free cash flow turns positive across the group.
3. **Depreciation arrives without damage.** As the 2025–2026 hardware runs through depreciation, margins hold.

If all three happen, the spending was an investment and I was early to worry. If none do, a lot of today's earnings were a timing difference. Early and wrong look identical for a while. I'll tell you which one I was.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction. These are the lines I'd read each earnings season, mostly in the footnotes, where the interesting things tend to live:

1. **Depreciation growth vs. revenue growth** at the big buyers. If depreciation starts growing faster than revenue, the bill is arriving.
2. **Debt and equity raised as a share of capex.** FactSet's 32% is the baseline. Up means the bet is getting more borrowed.
3. **Useful-life footnotes.** Any further lengthening of server lives lifts reported earnings without changing a dollar of cash. A shortening would be an admission that the chips age faster than the books said.
4. **AI revenue disclosures.** Companies that break out AI revenue are giving you the numerator. Companies that won't are asking you to trust the denominator.
5. **Memory makers' contracted share.** If Micron's and SanDisk's committed volumes start slipping, the buyers have blinked first.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"not have enough capacity to meet all the demand we have in 2026"* — Andy Jassy, Amazon CEO, Q2 2026 earnings call, July 31, 2026, as reported by [Data Center Knowledge](https://datacenterknowledge.com/infrastructure/amazon-lifts-ai-infrastructure-spending-to-220b-as-demand-outpaces-capacity). *(Verify against the transcript before publishing.)*

**Translation:** "We're going to spend even more."

**What it's hiding:** "Demand" here means demand at today's prices, for capacity being built today. It doesn't say whether that demand is profitable, how much comes from customers who are themselves spending borrowed or investor money, or what happens to pricing once the capacity shortage ends. Every shortage looks like a boom from inside. The question is what the boom looks like after the shortage is solved, when everyone has built for it at once.
