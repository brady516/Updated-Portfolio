# They're not selling you a car — they're selling you a payment

True story. Years ago, better credit than I have today, I sat down to buy a car
and the rep slid the **four-square** across the desk — that little grid with
trade-in, price, down payment, and monthly payment, all in play at once. It looks
like a worksheet. It's a magic trick. The whole design is to keep four numbers
moving so you never stop on the only two that matter: the **price** and the **APR**.

So I took out my **HP 10bII** — a calculator I knew cold from my MBA in finance —
and did the rude thing: I solved for the rate they'd actually buried in the payment.
It came out to **6.8%**. I slid it back and said — politely — "My credit doesn't
support nearly 7%. I want you to make your return. My counter, and it's final, is
**2.5%**." By the time the deal was done, the payment had gone from **$398** to
**$254** a month.

The rep said he'd sold cars for twenty years and had never watched someone take
the four-square apart that cleanly. I'll take it.

Here's the part that keeps me honest, though: I didn't invent that move. Years
earlier, back when I was a food-and-beverage exec, a financial "advisor" at
a big insurance company walked me through the four-square like he was handing me the
nuclear codes. I thought I'd been let in on something. (In hindsight, that
relationship was mostly about product, but that's a different issue.)

**I thought the four-square was an edge. It was the minimum to play.**

That gap — between what felt like an edge and what was actually just table stakes —
is the whole reason this newsletter exists. Most of what gets sold to regular
people as an *edge* (the clever tactic, the "sophisticated" product, the secret
the pros supposedly hoard) is the minimum to play, marked up and gift-wrapped. The
real edge was never a trick. It's refusing to be the person the trick is built for.

Quick aside on why this trick even exists: it's an American specialty, and not by
accident. In most of this country a car isn't a purchase, it's a permission slip —
to get to work, to school, to exist. The four-square is built on exactly that
leverage. When the buyer *needs* the thing to function in society, "what payment
works for you?" isn't a question. It's a frisk.

## Signal vs. Noise — "what monthly payment were you hoping for?" *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Let's just figure out what payment works for you and get you in
> this car today."

That question is the trap, asked warmly. Anchor on the monthly payment and they
have infinite room to hide a high rate or a long term behind a number that "feels"
fine. The payment is the wrapper. The rate and the price are the gift.

> **The Signal:** negotiate the **price** and the **APR** separately, in daylight.
> Treat the monthly payment as an *output* you compute yourself — never the input
> you hand them.

## The Number — 6.8% → 2.5% *(free)*

Here's the honest math, because this newsletter checks its own stories too. On the
same term, cutting the rate from 6.8% to 2.5% takes a $398 payment down to roughly
**$345–$366**, depending on the loan length. The rate alone doesn't explain the full
**$144 a month** (about $1,700 a year). The rest came out of the other squares once
they stopped moving together. That's the trick: four numbers in motion so you can't
tell which one is costing you. *(Illustrative: 48–84-month terms; rate-only effect.)*

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week — Dealership Edition *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"What can we do to get you into this vehicle today? Let's
work backward from a comfortable monthly number."*

**Translation:** "Please stop thinking about the interest rate."

**What it's hiding:** "work backward from the payment" is the entire con in seven
words — it makes the one number you can feel the anchor, and the two numbers that
cost you (rate, term) the adjustable background. Same move as a fund quoting
"income" and burying the risk. Different desk, identical sleight of hand.

## What I'd Watch — the $4 lesson *(free)*

Here's my favorite part, and it's on me, not the dealer. I called my wife with the
win — payment down to $254. She'd set a number in her head: **under $250.** And for
a moment, a deal that had just gotten *$144 a month* better was in genuine jeopardy
over the last **$4**.

That's anchoring, in the wild, in my own kitchen. We'd already won the war; the
brain still wanted to die on the hill of a round number. (We kept the car. I love
my wife. The point stands.)

The lesson isn't "be smarter than a car salesman." It's: **decide which number
actually matters before anyone — including you — picks one for you.** Walk in
solving for total cost and APR, bring the calculator, and let the monthly payment
be the thing that falls out of the math. The four-square only works on people who
agreed to play four-square.
