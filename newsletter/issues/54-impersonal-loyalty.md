# Impersonal Loyalty: Why Staying Put Became a Subsidy

## Intro *(free)*

Seventy years ago, loyalty to an insurance company meant something specific. A local agent knew your name, knew when you got married, and called you when your rate could come down. The relationship ran both ways.

I switched auto insurers this year after nearly a decade with the same company. On the way out, the representative told me, in so many words, that they hadn't known I was married, and I'd been eligible for a discount the whole time. Nobody had asked. Nobody had checked. The discount sat there unclaimed for years, and the system was perfectly comfortable with that.

That's what loyalty means now. Not a relationship. A database record that stopped being updated. This issue is about why that happens, who pays for it, and what would prove the whole thing overblown.

## Signal vs. Noise *(free)*

> **The Noise:** "Stay with us and we'll reward your loyalty."

The pitch is everywhere: tenure discounts, loyalty rewards, "valued customer since 2017." And some of it is real. Plenty of insurers do discount long-tenured customers.

The question is what the discount is measured against. Bob Hunter of the Consumer Federation of America put it bluntly in 2015: insurers will give you a 10 percent loyalty discount "after they've raised your rate 25 percent" ([NPR via WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/2015-05-08/being-a-loyal-auto-insurance-customer-can-cost-you), May 2015). A discount off an inflated base isn't a reward. It's a coupon on a markup.

The math nobody shows you is the comparison that matters: what you pay to renew versus what a brand-new customer with your exact risk would pay today. Your renewal notice never prints that number. It shows you last year's price, this year's price, and a reason for the change. The reference point that would tell you whether you're being rewarded or harvested is the one thing left off the page.

> **The Signal:** loyalty is only worth what it saves you against the price a stranger would pay for the same risk. If you don't know that number, you don't know whether you're being rewarded.

## The Number *(free)*

**12.6%.** That's the share of U.S. auto insurance customers who shopped for a new policy in the second quarter of 2026, according to J.D. Power and TransUnion. Only **4.5%** switched ([Repairer Driven News](https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/2026/08/07/auto-insurance-shopping-down-switching-up-in-q2/), Aug. 7, 2026).

Read it the other way around: in a given quarter, roughly seven of every eight drivers don't even look. That isn't a judgment about those drivers. Shopping takes time, policies are hard to compare, and most people assume their insurer is treating them fairly. But a market where most customers never check the price is a market where the price doesn't have to be competitive for most customers.

What would change its meaning: these are quarterly figures, and shopping rates move with premium shocks. J.D. Power's annual study found 53% of customers who requested a quote in the prior six months were actively shopping, down from 57% a year earlier ([J.D. Power 2026 U.S. Insurance Shopping Study](https://www.jdpower.com/sites/default/files/file/2026-06-02/2026033%20U.S.%20Insurance%20Shopping%20Study.pdf)). If premiums keep cooling and fewer people shop, inertia grows. If shopping stays elevated, the inertia discount shrinks.

## The Read — loyalty used to be a relationship; now it's a subsidy *(paid)*

**Thesis:** in a market where most customers don't shop, insurers have a structural incentive to let loyal customers' prices drift, and to fix them only when the customer threatens to leave. In my view, the customer who stays put helps pay for the discounts offered to the customers who shop.

### The mechanism has a name

The industry calls it **price optimization**. Maryland's insurance regulator defined it plainly in 2014 as "the practice of varying rates based on factors other than risk of loss." Its two examples: how likely a policyholder is to switch, and how willing a policyholder is to pay more than others. Maryland prohibited it for property and casualty insurers in Bulletin 14-23, issued October 31, 2014 ([Carlton Fields summary](https://www.carltonfields.com/insights/publications/2014/maryland-insurance-administration-prohibits-use-of)).

Read that definition again. The bulletin describes "switching behavior" as comparing quotes, canceling early, or letting a policy lapse. In other words, the regulator banned pricing you based on how likely you are to shop. The fact that the ban had to be written tells you the practice existed.

Maryland and Ohio were early movers, and California warned insurers against it ([NPR via WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/2015-05-08/being-a-loyal-auto-insurance-customer-can-cost-you)). The industry's own framing at the time came from the Insurance Information Institute's Robert Hartwig: "Insurance is sort of the original big data industry."

### What happens when someone actually measures it

The U.S. never produced a national measurement. The United Kingdom did. Its Financial Conduct Authority found that in 2018, about 6 million loyal home and motor policyholders could have collectively saved **£1.2 billion** by paying the average price for their actual risk. The FCA's response was blunt: starting January 1, 2022, insurers must offer renewing customers "a price that is no higher than they would pay as a new customer." It estimated the rule would save consumers **£4.2 billion over 10 years** ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-confirms-measures-protect-customers-loyalty-penalty-home-motor-insurance-markets), May 28, 2021).

That's the cleanest evidence available that the loyalty penalty isn't a conspiracy theory. A regulator measured it, sized it, and banned it.

### Why it survives even where it's banned

Here's the subtler part, and the reason I don't think my own story was price optimization. Maryland bans pricing on your likelihood to shop. It doesn't ban inertia.

My missing discount wasn't an algorithm deciding I was unlikely to leave. It was simpler: a life event that never made it into my file, and no process on the other side to go looking for it. The burden of auditing the policy sat entirely with me. The insurer had no reason to volunteer a discount, and every reason to wait.

That's the structural point. Even with price optimization banned, a market where customers rarely shop and rarely audit their own policies rewards the company for letting files go stale. Nobody has to break a rule. The incentive does the work.

### The fair case for the other side

Most premium changes are about risk, not loyalty. The last few years brought real increases in repair costs, parts, and claims severity, and those hit every customer. Tenure discounts are real at many carriers. Switching has real costs too: you can lose accident forgiveness, multi-policy bundles, or a claims relationship that works. And price isn't the only thing you're buying.

I'll put my own decision on the record here. When I left, my former insurer's corrected quote came in **about $2,000 a year** below what I now pay. I left anyway, on purpose, because I've lived through a claim with both companies and the service difference was worth the money to me. Loyalty to price is usually a loss. Loyalty to service, chosen deliberately and re-checked, is a decision.

### What would prove me wrong

1. **U.S. renewal prices track new-customer prices.** If a study comparing renewal quotes to new-business quotes for identical risk profiles finds no meaningful gap, the subsidy thesis fails for this market.
2. **Tenure discounts outweigh the drift.** If long-tenured customers, adjusted for risk, consistently pay *less* than new customers, loyalty is being rewarded, not harvested.
3. **The UK ban backfires.** If UK data shows the price-walking ban simply raised prices for new customers without lowering total costs, the "loyalty penalty" was a pricing transfer, not an overcharge, and the case for action weakens.

The honest bottom line: the U.S. evidence is thinner than the UK's, and I'm one data point. But the UK measured the penalty, Maryland banned the mechanism by name, and the shopping data says most people never check. That's enough for me to treat my renewal notice as an opening offer, not a statement of fact.

## What I'd Watch *(paid)*

Observation, not instruction:

1. **Usage-based insurance.** J.D. Power found 20% of customers now use telematics-based pricing, rising to 34% of those buying from a new insurer ([J.D. Power](https://www.jdpower.com/sites/default/files/file/2026-06-02/2026033%20U.S.%20Insurance%20Shopping%20Study.pdf)). More data means more precise pricing. The open question is whether the precision stays about risk.
2. **AI-driven pricing and the regulators watching it.** As pricing models get more complex, the line between "risk factor" and "willingness to pay" gets harder to audit from outside.
3. **The shopping-versus-switching gap.** If more people shop but the switching rate stays low, insurers are probably matching prices when customers threaten to leave. That's retention pricing, and it only reaches the customers who ask.
4. **Your own life events.** Marriage, a move, a new car, a paid-off loan, a better credit score. Each can change your price. None of them updates itself.
5. **Whether any U.S. state follows the UK's approach** and requires renewal prices to match new-customer prices outright.

## Wall Street Word Salad of the Week — Insurance Edition *(paid)*

**This week's salad:** *"We do not engage in price optimization that seeks to charge the highest rate…"* — amended statement from an Allstate spokesman to NPR, 2015 ([NPR via WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/2015-05-08/being-a-loyal-auto-insurance-customer-can-cost-you)).

**Translation:** "We don't do the most extreme version of the thing."

**What it's hiding:** The qualifier does all the work. "Price optimization *that seeks to charge the highest rate the market will bear*" is a narrower thing than price optimization. According to NPR, the company's first answer was a flat denial, and a 2011 SEC filing had said it used the practice. The amended sentence is technically careful and practically meaningless: it rules out the cartoon version and leaves everything else on the table.
