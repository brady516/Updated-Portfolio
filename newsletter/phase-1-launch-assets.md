Years ago I sat down to buy a car with better credit than I have today, and the
salesman slid the **four-square** across the desk.

If you've never seen it: it's a little grid — trade-in, price, down payment,
monthly payment — with all four numbers in play at once. It looks like a
worksheet. It's a magic trick. The entire design is to keep four numbers moving
so you never stop on the only two that matter: **the price and the APR.**

So I did the rude thing. I took out my HP 10bII — a calculator I knew cold from
an MBA in investments — and solved for the interest rate they'd buried in the
monthly payment.

**6.8%.**

I slid it back and said, politely: "My credit doesn't support nearly 7%. I want
you to make your return. My counter is final — 2.5%."

Payment went from **$398 to $254 a month.** Same car. The car didn't get cheaper.
The *story about the car* did.

The rep said he'd sold cars for twenty years and never watched someone take the
four-square apart that cleanly. I'll take it.

But here's the part that keeps me honest: I didn't invent that move. Years
earlier a financial "advisor" walked me through the same four-square like he was
handing me the nuclear codes. I thought I'd been let in on a secret.

**I thought it was an edge. It was the minimum to play.**

That gap — between what *feels* like an edge and what's actually just table
stakes, marked up and gift-wrapped — is the whole reason I'm starting a
newsletter. Most of what gets sold to regular people as sophistication is table
stakes with a bow on it. The real edge was never a trick. It's refusing to be the
person the trick is built for.

It's called **North de Noise.** Institutional-grade research, plainly stated, from
someone whose record you can actually check (FINRA BrokerCheck, CRD 6510444).
99% of finance content is noise. This is the other 1%.

First issue's live. Link in the comments.
